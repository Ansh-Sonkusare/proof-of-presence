Abstract—Manual and semi-automated attendance recording
methods used in most academic institutions remain slow, error-
prone, and highly susceptible to proxy attendance, where one
student marks presence on behalf of an absent peer. Existing
digital alternatives such as barcode scanning, Radio Frequency
Identification (RFID), and biometric recognition improve speed
but introduce new weaknesses: RFID cards and barcodes can be
freely exchanged between students, and biometric hardware is
expensive and difficult to scale across large classrooms. Simple
Quick Response (QR) code based attendance systems solve the
speed problem but remain trivially exploitable because a single
photograph of the projected QR code can be forwarded to
and scanned by an absent student from anywhere. This paper
proposes a Secure QR-Based Smart Attendance Tracking System
that combines a single dynamically-refreshed classroom QR
code with three independent anti-proxy layers: (1) a public-key
cryptographic signing scheme in which every enrolled student
possesses a unique asymmetric key pair generated and stored
inside the device’s secure hardware keystore, so that although the
entire class scans the same QR code, each student’s submitted
attendance token is individually and verifiably signed with a
private key that cannot be shared or reused by another student; (2)
a geofencing module that validates the Global Positioning System
(GPS) coordinates captured at the moment of scanning against
the registered geographic boundary of the classroom using the
Haversine distance formula; and (3) a device-binding policy that
restricts each student account to exactly one authenticated device,
invalidating any attempt to install the credentials on a second
phone. The server additionally enforces a short-lived, single-use
session nonce to prevent replay attacks. A working prototype was
implemented using a React Native mobile client, a Node.js/Express
verification server, Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm
(ECDSA) over the P-256 curve for signing, and a PostgreSQL
backend. Experimental evaluation across simulated classroom
sessions shows a proxy-attempt detection rate of 97.2%, an average
attendance-marking latency of 2.1 seconds per student, and zero
successful replay or spoofing attempts across 500 controlled trials,
substantially outperforming manual, RFID, and unsigned QR
baselines. The results demonstrate that combining cryptographic
non-repudiation with location and device constraints yields a
lightweight, scalable, and tamper-resistant attendance solution
suitable for real classroom deployment.

I. INTRODUCTION
Attendance monitoring is a routine but administratively
expensive process in every educational institution. Conventional
roll-call methods consume a considerable fraction of classroom
time, are prone to human error, and are frequently manipulated
through proxy attendance, in which a present student verbally
answers or signs on behalf of an absent classmate [1]. As
student populations grow and institutions adopt continuous
internal assessment policies that weight attendance heavily, the
demand for a fast, accurate, and tamper-resistant attendance
mechanism has become pressing [2].
A range of automated alternatives has been explored in
the literature, including barcode and RFID card scanning,
fingerprint and facial biometric recognition, Bluetooth Low
Energy (BLE) beacon proximity detection, and Quick Response
(QR) code scanning [3]. Among these, QR-code-based systems
have gained particular popularity because they require no
additional hardware beyond a smartphone camera and a printed
or projected code, and they integrate naturally with existing
learning management systems [4]. However, conventional
QR attendance implementations suffer from a fundamental
weakness: the QR code is a static, publicly visible artifact.
Once displayed, it can be photographed and instantly forwarded
through messaging applications to any absent student, who
can then scan the shared image from an arbitrary location
and be marked present without ever entering the classroom
[5]. Time-limited or periodically refreshed QR codes mitigate
replay to some extent but do not, by themselves, verify who is
scanning or where the scan physically occurred, nor do they
prevent one device from being used to mark attendance for
several accounts in succession [6].
Biometric and RFID-based systems address the identity-
binding problem more directly but introduce their own limita-
tions. Biometric sensors require dedicated hardware at every
classroom entrance, raise privacy concerns around the storage of
fingerprint or facial data, and are difficult to scale in institutions
with large lecture halls or in blended in-person/remote settings
[7]. RFID cards, like QR codes, are physical or digital tokens
that can simply be handed to another person, meaning that
possession of the token does not prove the presence of its
rightful owner [8].
This paper proposes a hybrid architecture that retains the low-
cost, hardware-free convenience of QR-code scanning while
closing its core security gap through cryptographic identity
binding. The key insight is that the classroom can continue to
display a single, shared QR code – avoiding the operational
overhead of generating and displaying a unique code per student
– provided that the response each student sends back to the
server after scanning is individually signed using a private
key that only that student’s enrolled device possesses. Because
Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA) signatures
are computationally infeasible to forge without knowledge of
the corresponding private key [9], this design guarantees that
even though every student in the room scans an identical QR
payload, the resulting attendance record submitted to the server
is cryptographically unique to each individual and cannot be
produced by anyone else – including someone who has merely
obtained a screenshot of the QR code.
Three complementary layers are combined to defend against
the realistic ways a determined student might still attempt to
circumvent the system:
1) Cryptographic token signing – every attendance submis-
sion must carry a valid ECDSA signature over a payload
containing the student ID, session nonce, timestamp,
and location, verified against a public key registered
at enrollment time. This prevents a student from ever
submitting a valid attendance record on behalf of another
student, since doing so would require possession of that
student’s private signing key.
2) Geofencing – the GPS coordinates captured on the
scanning device at the moment of signing must fall
within a configurable radius of the registered classroom
location, computed using the Haversine great-circle
distance formula [10]. This prevents an absent student
from marking attendance remotely even if they receive
a forwarded copy of the QR code image.
3) Device binding with single active session – each
student account is cryptographically bound to exactly one
physical device at a time; the private key never leaves the
device’s secure hardware keystore (Android Keystore or
iOS Secure Enclave) [11], and any attempt to re-register
the account on a second device revokes the first. This
prevents a single student from holding multiple accounts’
keys on one phone and marking attendance for several
classmates in sequence.

II. RELATED WORK
Automated attendance tracking has attracted substantial
research attention over the past decade, with proposed so-
lutions broadly falling into four categories: biometric systems,
RFID/NFC systems, location and proximity-based systems, and
QR-code systems.
Biometric attendance systems using fingerprint or facial
recognition have been widely studied for their strong identity-
binding properties, since a biometric trait cannot easily be
transferred to another individual [7]. However, such systems
require dedicated capture hardware at fixed points of entry,
incur significant deployment and maintenance cost across
multiple classrooms, and raise data-protection concerns because
biometric templates are highly sensitive personal information
that, once compromised, cannot be reissued the way a password
or key can [12]. Facial-recognition approaches in particular have
also been shown to degrade in accuracy under poor lighting,
partial occlusion (e.g., face masks), and crowded classroom
conditions [13].
RFID and NFC-based systems replace manual roll-call with a
tap-and-scan interaction and have demonstrated fast throughput
in controlled trials [8]. Their principal weakness, shared with
early QR implementations, is that the RFID tag or NFC-enabled
card is a bearer token: whoever physically holds the card is
recognized as the enrolled student, so cards are readily lent to
absent classmates. Some works have proposed combining RFID
with photograph verification by a proctor, but this reintroduces
manual overhead and does not scale to large lecture halls [14].
Location and proximity-based systems use Bluetooth Low
Energy (BLE) beacons, Wi-Fi fingerprinting, or GPS to infer
that a student’s device is physically present in the classroom
[15]. These approaches are effective against remote proxy
attempts but, used alone, do not verify which student is carrying
the device – a phone can be carried into the room by one student
and used to check in a friend who is not present, or a device’s
location can be spoofed using mock-location tools available
on rooted or jailbroken phones [16].
QR-code-based attendance has been proposed repeatedly
because it requires no additional hardware beyond a smartphone
camera [4] [5]. Early implementations displayed a single
static QR code for the full class duration, which is trivially
defeated by photographing and forwarding the code. Later work
introduced time-limited, periodically regenerated QR codes to
reduce the replay window [6], and some systems layered a
device fingerprint or IP-address check on top of the scan event
[17]. However, a periodically refreshed QR code alone still
does not cryptographically bind the resulting attendance record
to a specific, verified individual: any device that can decode the
currently displayed code within its validity window can submit
an attendance request under any student identity known to it,
unless the submission itself carries proof of the submitter’s
identity.
Separately, the use of public-key cryptography and digital
signatures for mobile authentication and non-repudiation has a
long research history in secure messaging, mobile banking, and
Internet-of-Things (IoT) device authentication [9] [18]. Modern
mobile operating systems expose hardware-backed secure key
storage (Android Keystore, iOS Secure Enclave) that generates
and stores private keys in isolated, non-exportable hardware,
making key extraction infeasible even on a compromised device
[11]. Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA)
over curves such as P-256 or Ed25519 is favored in resource-
constrained mobile and IoT contexts because it offers security
equivalent to much larger RSA keys with substantially smaller
signatures and faster computation [19], making it well suited
to a real-time classroom check-in scenario where signing and
verification must complete within a few hundred milliseconds.
Nonce-based replay protection, well established in authentica-
tion protocol design [20], is likewise a natural fit for preventing
a captured QR payload or a previously valid signed token from
being resubmitted after the fact.
To date, however, no widely reported classroom attendance
system combines all three properties – a single shared QR
artifact, per-student cryptographic non-repudiation via device-
held private keys, and simultaneous geofence and device-
binding enforcement – into one architecture. Table I summarizes
representative prior approaches against these criteria and
positions the proposed system relative to them.

III. PROPOSED SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
A. Overview
The proposed system is organized as a client-server archi-
tecture consisting of four principal actors: the Faculty/Session
Controller, which initiates and closes attendance sessions; the
Classroom QR Display, a projector or shared screen rendering a
dynamically refreshed QR code; the Student Mobile Application,
installed on each student’s registered device, which scans the
QR code, captures contextual data, and produces a digitally
signed attendance token; and the Verification Server, which
independently validates every submitted token against four
checks – signature authenticity, geofence compliance, device-
binding status, and nonce freshness – before committing
an attendance record to the database. Fig. 1 illustrates the
overall system architecture and the flow of data between these
components.
B. System Components
• Enrollment Service: On first use, the mobile application
generates an ECDSA (P-256) key pair inside the device’s
hardware-backed keystore. The private key is marked
non-exportable so that it can never leave the secure
hardware element, even if the device’s operating system
is compromised. Only the public key, together with the
student’s institutional ID and a device fingerprint, is
transmitted to the server and stored in the Public Key
Registry.
• Session Controller: A faculty-facing interface used to
start and stop attendance sessions for a given class, lecture
slot, and room. On session start, the server creates a
session record containing a unique session identifier, the
registered GPS coordinates and permissible radius of the
room, and a validity window (typically 60–90 seconds
per QR refresh).
• Classroom QR Display: Renders a QR code encoding
the current session identifier, a freshly generated random
nonce, and an expiry timestamp. The displayed code is

Each session’s QR code is deliberately kept identical for

hundreds of students. Uniqueness and non-repudiation are
instead achieved entirely at the response stage: because each
student’s device independently signs the shared session data
with its own private key, the resulting tokens submitted to the
server are all distinct and individually attributable, even though
they originate from a single displayed QR payload. This design
is the central architectural contribution of the present work and
is elaborated in Section IV.
IV. CRYPTOGRAPHIC TOKEN DESIGN AND DIGITAL
SIGNATURE SCHEME
The core anti-proxy guarantee of the proposed system rests
on asymmetric-key digital signatures. This section describes key
generation, token structure, the signing algorithm, server-side
verification, and the replay-prevention mechanism.
A. Key Pair Generation and Secure Storage
During enrollment, each student’s mobile application invokes
the platform’s hardware-backed keystore (Android Keystore
or iOS Secure Enclave) to generate an ECDSA key pair over
the NIST P-256 curve. Let G denote the base point of the
curve of order n. The device selects a random private key
d∈[1,n−1] and computes the corresponding public key as
the elliptic-curve point
Q= d·G. (1)
The private key d is generated and retained exclusively inside
secure hardware; the operating system, application code, and
any backend service never observe its value, and the keystore
API refuses all export requests. Only the public key Q is
transmitted to the Verification Server, where it is stored in
the Public Key Registry indexed by student ID and bound to
the single device identifier permitted for that account (Section
VI). This design ensures that even a fully rooted or reverse-
engineered client application cannot be used to extract another
student’s signing key, since the key material never exists outside
the secure hardware boundary of its owner’s device [11].

C. Signing Process
The device first hashes the serialized payload using SHA-
256:
H = SHA256(M). (2)
To produce a signature, the device generates a cryptographically
secure random per-signature value k∈[1,n−1] and computes
the elliptic-curve point
R= k·G= (xR,yR), r= xR mod n. (3)
If r= 0, a new k is drawn. The signature component sis then
computed as
s= k−1 (H+ r·d) mod n. (4)
If s= 0, a new k is likewise drawn. The resulting signature
(r,s) is appended to the payload M and transmitted to the
server as the attendance token T= {M,r,s}.
D. Server-Side Verification
Upon receiving T, the server retrieves the public key Q
registered for student_id and confirms 1 ≤r,s≤n−1.
It recomputes H = SHA256(M) and calculates
w= s−1 mod n, u1 = H·wmod n, u2 = r·wmod n.
(5)
It then computes the curve point
P= u1·G+ u2·Q= (xP ,yP ). (6)
The signature is accepted if and only if
xP mod n= r. (7)
Because Q= d·G, this equality holds precisely when the
signature was produced using the private key dcorresponding to
the registered Q, which by construction resides only inside the
enrolled student’s own device. Consequently, no other student
– even one who has captured a screenshot of the classroom QR
code and knows the current session identifier and nonce – can
produce a token that passes Eq. (7) without also possessing
the target student’s private key, which never leaves secure
hardware.
E. Replay and Reuse Prevention
Each session nonce embedded in the QR code is single-
use: upon the first successful verification of a token refer-
encing a given (session_id, nonce) pair for a given
student_id, the server marks that pair as consumed in a
fast in-memory store. Any subsequent submission referencing
the same nonce – whether from the original student attempting
to game the system or from a captured and resubmitted token
– is rejected outright. Because the QR code itself refreshes
its nonce every 60–90 seconds, the window during which a
captured code image remains submittable is short, and because
submission additionally requires a valid signature bound to
the submitting student’s own key, capturing the QR image
confers no benefit to an absent student regardless of the refresh
interval.
V. LOCATION-BASED PROXY PREVENTION VIA
GEOFENCING
While the signature scheme in Section IV guarantees that
a submitted token could only have been produced by the
legitimate owner of the corresponding private key, it does
not by itself guarantee that the student was physically present
in the classroom – a student could, in principle, sign a valid
token from a remote location if the QR payload were somehow
relayed to them in real time. Geofencing closes this residual
gap by additionally requiring that the GPS coordinates captured
at signing time fall within the registered classroom boundary.
A. Haversine-Based Geofence Validation
Let (φ1,λ1) denote the registered latitude/longitude of
the classroom and (φ2,λ2) the coordinates captured by the
student’s device at scan time. The great-circle distance d
between the two points is computed using the Haversine
formula:

where R ≈6371 km is the mean radius of the Earth. The

B. Mitigating Location Spoofing
Because mock-location tools on rooted or jailbroken devices
can, in principle, report falsified GPS coordinates, the mobile
application additionally queries the operating system’s mock-
location and developer-options flags and rejects the scan locally
if a fake-location provider is detected. Combined with device-
binding (Section VI), which limits an account to a single
previously-vetted device, this substantially raises the effort
required to defeat the geofence check compared to a naive
GPS-only design, since a spoofed location must originate from
the specific enrolled device rather than any arbitrary phone.
VI. DEVICE BINDING AND SESSION MANAGEMENT
The third defensive layer restricts each student account
to a single actively authenticated device. At enrollment, the
application computes a device fingerprint from a combination
of hardware identifiers and the freshly generated public key
Q, and registers this fingerprint against the student’s account.
The server subsequently accepts signed tokens for that account
only from the registered fingerprint.
If a student attempts to install the application and re-enroll
on a second device, the server invalidates the public key and
device binding associated with the first device before accepting
the new registration, and the change is logged and optionally
flagged for administrator review if it occurs during an active
or upcoming session window. This design directly prevents the
scenario in which a single student installs multiple classmates’
accounts (and their associated private keys) on one physical
phone in order to mark several students present in sequence:
because each account’s private key is generated inside, and non-
exportable from, its own enrolled device’s secure hardware,
an account’s key material cannot be copied onto a second
device at all – re-enrollment instead generates an entirely new
key pair, immediately invalidating the student’s ability to be
recognized under their old credentials, and any concentration
of many distinct accounts’ active bindings on one device is
visible to administrators through the device-fingerprint audit
log.
VII. IMPLEMENTATION
A working prototype was built to validate the architecture
described above. The student-facing client was implemented
in React Native, targeting both Android and iOS, using the
platform Keystore/Secure Enclave APIs for ECDSA P-256
key generation and signing, and the device camera for QR
decoding. The verification server was implemented in Node.js
with the Express framework, exposing a RESTful API secured
with TLS 1.3. PostgreSQL was used for persistent storage of
student records, public keys, device bindings, and attendance
logs, while a Redis in-memory store was used for fast nonce-
consumption tracking to support sub-second replay checks

VIII. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
The prototype was evaluated across simulated classroom
sessions of 60 students each, comparing the proposed system
against four baselines: manual roll call, RFID card scanning,
an unsigned time-limited QR code system, and a fingerprint
biometric system. Each configuration was subjected to con-
trolled proxy-attempt trials, in which a subset of “absent”
participants attempted to be marked present using forwarded
QR images, borrowed RFID cards, or remote GPS-spoofed
requests, depending on the method under test.
As summarized in Table II, the proposed system achieved
the highest proxy-attempt detection rate (97.2%) among all
evaluated methods while also recording the lowest average
per-student marking time (2.1 s), since the scan-sign-submit
flow completes almost instantly on modern mobile hardware.
The residual 2.8% of proxy attempts that evaded detection
corresponded to edge cases where a student’s own registered
device was carried into the classroom by a friend and used
with the true owner briefly nearby to satisfy the geofence check
– a scenario that lies outside the threat model addressable by
any purely digital signal and would require in-person spot
verification to fully eliminate.

IX. SECURITY THREAT ANALYSIS
Table IV summarizes the principal attack vectors considered
during system design, together with the specific mechanism
used to mitigate each.
It is important to note that the system’s guarantees are
probabilistic rather than absolute in one specific residual case:
a legitimately enrolled student’s own device, carried physically
into the classroom, could be used to sign a valid token while
that student is momentarily not the one performing the scan
action – for example, if a friend holds the phone. Because
this scenario requires the device (and hence the still-attached
secure-hardware private key) to be physically present within
the classroom geofence at scan time, it does not enable remote
proxying, and its scope is limited to brief in-person hand-offs,
which future work (Section X) proposes to further constrain
through liveness cues such as an on-device selfie challenge
captured at the moment of signing.
X. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presented a secure QR-code-based smart atten-
dance tracking system that addresses the long-standing proxy-
attendance problem through a layered architecture combining
public-key digital signatures, GPS-based geofencing, and
single-device account binding. The central contribution is a
cryptographic token design in which a single, shared classroom
QR code produces individually verifiable and non-forgeable
attendance records, since each student’s device signs the
shared session data with a private key generated and retained
exclusively inside secure device hardware – eliminating the
need to generate, distribute, or manage a separate QR code per
student while still guaranteeing per-student non-repudiation.

The Haversine-based geofence check further ensures that only
devices physically located within the registered classroom
boundary can complete a valid submission, and the device-
binding policy prevents a single phone from being used to
mark attendance for multiple accounts. Prototype evaluation
demonstrated a 97.2% proxy-attempt detection rate, a 2.1-
second average marking time, and complete elimination of
replayed-token acceptance across controlled trials, substantially
outperforming manual, RFID, unsigned-QR, and biometric
baselines on the combined metrics of speed and tamper
resistance.
Future work will explore several extensions: (1) incorporating
a lightweight on-device liveness or selfie challenge at the
moment of signing to further constrain the brief in-person
hand-off scenario identified in Section IX; (2) evaluating post-
quantum signature schemes as a forward-looking replacement
for ECDSA in anticipation of future cryptographic threats;
(3) extending the geofencing model with indoor position-
ing techniques (Wi-Fi Round-Trip-Time or Ultra-Wideband
ranging) to improve accuracy in multi-story buildings where
GPS reception is degraded; and (4) conducting a longitudinal
deployment study across a full academic semester to evaluate
real-world robustness, user acceptance, and administrative
workload reduction at scale.
