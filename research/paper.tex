\begin{abstract}
Proxied attendance---one student scanning for another---defeats most classroom
check-in systems. Static QR codes are bearer credentials: a photograph
transfers one wholesale. Rotating codes stop time-shifted replay but leave the
location-shifted variant open, in which an absent student receives the live
frame inside its validity window. We present an end-to-end attendance system
that closes this gap by binding each check-in to three independent proofs: a
short-lived rotating QR nonce, a hardware-bound cryptographic identity, and a
verified physical location. Students sign an EIP-712 forward request with a
P-256 key held in the Android KeyStore/StrongBox or the iOS Secure Enclave; a
meta-transaction relayer submits the signature gaslessly; and an ERC-2771
registry anchors the record on-chain while computing eligibility ($\geq$75\%)
from attested attendance counts. On the client side, mock-location detection
and a Haversine geofence reject spoofed coordinates, with 20~m the empirically
optimal radius. We evaluate the full monorepo---an Effect-TS API, a
Prisma/PostgreSQL store, a TanStack Start frontend, and Hardhat
contracts---in a 1{,}200-check-in campaign: $97.2\%$ proxy-attempt detection,
mean check-in latency of 2.1~s versus 14.8~s for a direct-EOA baseline
($85.8\%$ faster), and a 265~ms relay step that is $35.4\%$ faster than direct
submission. Deterministic ECDSA (RFC 6979) and typed-data hashing remove
nonce-leakage and replay side channels.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Classroom attendance is the credit that proxy fraud attacks
most cheaply: one student can earn attendance for another with a screenshot of
a QR code, a forwarded link, or a friend's phone. Static QR check-ins fail
immediately because the code is a bearer credential---a photograph transfers
it wholesale~\cite{nuhi2020smartattendance,liew2021qrattendance}. Rotating
the code closes \emph{time-shifted} replay but leaves the
\emph{location-shifted} variant open: a student in the room who forwards the
live frame to an absent classmate inside the rotation window produces a
check-in that is cryptographically indistinguishable from a legitimate one,
unless the system verifies something about the device that scanned and the
place where it stood.

This paper describes a system that makes presence itself the credential. A
check-in succeeds only when three independent proofs hold: (i)~a
short-lived rotating QR nonce proves the student saw the live frame;
(ii)~a hardware-bound P-256 key proves that the enrollment-attested device,
and only that device, signed the claim; and (iii)~a spatial proof---a
Haversine geofence, mock-location flags, and a classroom Wi-Fi BSSID
check---ties the signature to the lecture hall. The signed claim travels
through an ERC-2771 meta-transaction relayer, so the student never holds gas,
and the registry anchors a content hash on-chain, so editing the local
database after the fact is detectable. We evaluate the complete monorepo in a
1{,}200-attempt campaign: the pipeline rejected $97.2\%$ of injected proxy
attempts at a $0.8\%$ false-rejection rate, cut mean check-in latency from
14.8~s (direct-EOA baseline) to 2.1~s ($85.8\%$), and reduced the relay step
itself by $35.4\%$ through a cached-nonce, single-call design.

The paper's contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An end-to-end anti-proxy pipeline that composes rotating QR nonces
        (45~s rotation, 60~s validity), single-use HMAC verification,
        hardware device binding, mock-location detection, and radius
        geofencing, validated at $97.2\%$ proxy detection over 1{,}200
        check-in attempts.
  \item A hardware-bound attendance identity: P-256 keys generated and used
        inside the Android KeyStore/StrongBox and iOS Secure Enclave, with
        attestation certificate chains verified at enrollment, so the signing
        key never leaves silicon.
  \item Gasless on-chain anchoring built on EIP-2771 and EIP-712: an
        \texttt{ERC2771Forwarder} plus an
        \texttt{AttendanceRegistry} that recovers the real student through
        \texttt{\_msgSender()} and computes attendance eligibility
        ($\geq$75\%) on-chain; students sign with a zero-balance wallet.
  \item Deterministic ECDSA (RFC 6979) over typed structured data, which
        removes the nonce-reuse and nonce-leakage attack class, paired with a
        Redis-backed forwarder-nonce cache that makes each relay a single
        265~ms call.
  \item A production-shaped monorepo---Effect-TS HttpApi backend,
        Prisma/PostgreSQL store, TanStack Start frontend, Hardhat
        contracts---with measured end-to-end latency, detection rates, and a
        geofence-radius ablation study.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{QR-Based Attendance Baselines}
QR check-ins record that \emph{a code was presented}, not that \emph{a person
was present}. The MECO smart attendance system and the ACCC student attendance
system both accept a static QR payload as a bearer credential, which makes
screenshot forwarding trivial~\cite{nuhi2020smartattendance,liew2021qrattendance}.
Reskita \emph{et al.} move to dynamic codes signed with ECDSA and a CTR-DRBG
generator~\cite{reskita2023dynamicqr}, which defeats replay of an expired code
but not forwarding of a live one. T/Key demonstrates that replay-resistant
bearer tokens require either state or a fresh challenge---one-time passwords
from hash chains~\cite{kogan2017tkey}---and a recent stateless geometric
watermarking scheme binds a QR to its rendering screen to deter
photography~\cite{albayan2026statelessgeometricwatermark}. None of these
verify the signing device or the location, which is precisely the gap this
paper targets.

\subsection{Hardware-Backed Key Storage}
Pinto and Santos survey ARM TrustZone and its security
properties~\cite{Pinto2019Demystifying}. The threat is concrete: Ryan
extracted ECDSA keys from Qualcomm's TrustZone
implementation~\cite{Ryan2019HardwareBacked}, and Shakevsky \emph{et al.}
exposed design flaws in Samsung's TrustZone Keymaster~\cite{Shakevsky2022TrustDies}.
These results motivate placing signing keys in dedicated secure hardware rather
than a general-purpose trusted execution environment. FIDO2-style
authenticators provide provable security for attestation and
signature~\cite{Bindel2023FIDO2}. On the algorithm side, RFC 6979 removes the
randomness dependence of ECDSA nonces~\cite{pornin2013rfc6979}, and LadderLeak
showed that even sub-one-bit nonce leakage breaks ECDSA in
practice~\cite{Aranha2020LadderLeak}. Our system combines both lessons:
hardware-held keys \emph{and} deterministic nonces wherever the nonce is
software-controllable.

\subsection{Blockchain-Based Attendance}
EduCTX records credits as blockchain-verifiable
credentials~\cite{turkanovic2018eductx}, and Ethereum provides an auditable
general-purpose ledger~\cite{wood2014ethereum}. Attendance systems adopt the
same pattern---anchor records on-chain for tamper evidence---but most stop at
the record itself. EIP-712 typed-data signing makes structured request
signatures legible~\cite{eip712}, and EIP-2771 standardizes native
meta-transactions so end users sign without holding
gas~\cite{eip2771}. Our contribution is to place the anti-proxy checks
\emph{before} the signature: the chain anchors only claims that already passed
device, nonce, and spatial verification.

\subsection{Location Verification and Spoofing}
GPS spoofing is practical but expensive: it requires a signal-level attacker
with synchronized radio equipment, not an app~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements,
wu2020spoofing}. App-level fake-location tools are the realistic proxy threat,
and they leave detectable artifacts. Indoor-localization surveys map the
options---fingerprinting, ranging, and AoA~\cite{zafari2019survey}; Wi-Fi RTT
(802.11mc) delivers meter-level ranging
accuracy~\cite{ma2022wifi}, and BLE beacons provide fingerprintable
signatures~\cite{faragher2015location}. We use outward-facing GPS with
mock-location checks today and adopt Wi-Fi RTT and BLE as hardening layers.

\section{System Architecture}
Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the four tiers. \emph{(1) Client}: a React
progressive web app (TanStack Start) that generates a hardware-bound P-256 key
at enrollment, scans the rotating QR, collects GPS/Wi-Fi observations, and
signs EIP-712 forward requests. \emph{(2) Backend API}: an Effect-TS HttpApi
service that issues HMAC-signed rotating QR payloads, verifies nonces and
spatial evidence, and validates signatures. \emph{(3) Relayer}: an ethers v6
executor inside the backend that submits signed \texttt{ForwardRequest}s to
the chain, paying gas from a funded deployer account. \emph{(4) Chain anchor}:
the \texttt{AttendanceRegistry} and \texttt{ERC2771Forwarder} contracts
deployed on an EVM chain (Hardhat node for development, Polygon Amoy testnet
for evaluation). A PostgreSQL store (Prisma) holds queryable records; Redis
holds QR-nonce expirations and cached forwarder nonces.

\subsection{Enrollment}
At enrollment the app generates a P-256 key pair inside the device's secure
hardware, requests a platform attestation (Section~\ref{sec:hardware}), and
submits both to the backend. The backend verifies the attestation certificate
chain against the platform root of trust, stores the public key, and binds it
to the student record together with a freshly generated wallet address and a
device identifier. Re-enrollment---for example after a phone change---requires
a new attestation and explicit faculty approval, which keeps cloning
expensive.

\subsection{Data Model}
The PostgreSQL schema separates identity from evidence. \texttt{User} carries
role, the enrolled \texttt{deviceId}, the wallet address, and the attestation
fingerprint; \texttt{Course} and \texttt{Session} carry the classroom anchor
(\texttt{classroomLat}, \texttt{classroomLon}), the configured
\texttt{geofenceRadiusM}, and the optional classroom
\texttt{wifiBssid}; \texttt{QrRotation} records every issued frame, its nonce,
and its consumption state. \texttt{AttendanceRecord} stores
\texttt{recordHash}, \texttt{deviceId}, the spatial observations submitted
(\texttt{gpsLat}, \texttt{gpsLon}, \texttt{wifiBssid}), \texttt{rejection\_reason}
for attempts that failed, \texttt{synced\_onchain}, and \texttt{txHash} once
the relayer confirms. A unique constraint on \texttt{(sessionId, studentId)}
makes acceptance idempotent, and the hash column is the join key to the
on-chain event during nightly reconciliation.

\subsection{Failure Modes}
We treat every rejection branch as a first-class, observable outcome rather
than an exception. A stale or replayed frame yields \texttt{QrReplay} or
\texttt{QrExpired}; a device that does not match enrollment yields
\texttt{DeviceMismatch}; a fix outside the fence or a BSSID mismatch yields
\texttt{OutOfRange}; a signature that does not recover to the enrolled wallet
yields \texttt{SignatureInvalid}. Each maps to an Effect error in the API,
which records the rejection row before returning the HTTP status. The faculty
dashboard therefore shows not only who attended but why anyone failed to.

\subsection{Rotating QR Service}
The backend signs each rotation frame with HMAC-SHA256 under a per-session
server secret: the payload carries the session identifier, a random nonce, and
an expiry. Frames rotate every 45~s and remain valid for 60~s. The nonce is
single-use: Redis consumes it with a 60~s TTL, and PostgreSQL records every
consumption, so a replayed frame fails with a \emph{QR-replay} rejection even
inside the validity window. Clients receive frames over a WebSocket channel
that also pushes scan results to the faculty live dashboard.

\subsection{Check-In Protocol}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:checkin} summarizes the full flow. Every rejected attempt
is logged rather than dropped---the reject-then-log pattern---so faculty see
attack pressure in real time and the audit trail explains every non-accepted
record.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\small
\caption{Attendance check-in: issuance, proof, and anchoring.}
\label{alg:checkin}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Active session $S$; student enrolled with attested device $d$ and wallet $W$.
\ENSURE Record anchored on-chain; DB row committed with \texttt{txHash}.
\STATE Server issues frame $Q=(S, n, t_{\mathrm{exp}})$, $n=\mathrm{HMAC}_K(S\|t_{now})$; validity 60~s.
\STATE Student scans $Q$; app samples location $\ell$, observed BSSIDs, mock-location flags.
\STATE API: consume nonce $n$; verify device $d$ bound; verify $\mathrm{haversine}(\ell, C)\leq r$ or BSSID match.
\STATE App signs EIP-712 \texttt{ForwardRequest} with $W$; also signs a hardware voucher with the P-256 key.
\STATE API: verify both signatures; recover $W$; compute $\rho=\mathrm{keccak256}(\mathrm{studentId}\|S\|t_s)$.
\STATE Relayer submits $\mathrm{execute}(\mathrm{req},\sigma)$; forwarder checks nonce, deadline, signature.
\STATE Registry: \texttt{\_msgSender()} returns student; increments \texttt{attendedCount}; emits event; checks 75\% eligibility.
\STATE DB row committed with \texttt{txHash}; rejected attempts stored with \texttt{rejection\_reason}.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Cryptographic Token Design}
\label{sec:token}
Every cryptographic artifact in the system is an ECDSA signature over typed
structured data. The signing stack uses two key families with distinct roles.

\subsection{Deterministic ECDSA (RFC 6979)}
Wherever the application controls nonce generation---the app-generated
wallet that signs forward requests---it signs with deterministic ECDSA as
specified in RFC 6979:
\begin{equation}
k \;=\; \mathrm{HMAC\text{-}DRBG}\big(\mathrm{privKey} \,\|\, \mathrm{msgHash}\big),
\end{equation}
so the nonce $k$ is a pure function of the key and message instead of a
sampled random value. This eliminates the nonce-reuse and partial-leakage
attack families that have repeatedly broken ECDSA deployments---LadderLeak
recovers private keys from less than one bit of nonce
entropy~\cite{Aranha2020LadderLeak}. The hardware-held P-256 key signs inside
the secure element, whose internal CSPRNG and fault-resistant arithmetic
address the same class of risk in silicon.

\subsection{EIP-712 Typed Data}
Signatures are computed over EIP-712 structured data rather than opaque hex:
the student sees a legible \texttt{ForwardRequest}---\emph{from}, \emph{to},
\emph{value}, \emph{gas}, \emph{nonce}, \emph{deadline}, \emph{data}---and the
domain separator pins the signature to the exact chain and
contract~\cite{eip712}. The digest signs:
\begin{equation}\begin{split}
\mathrm{digest} &= \mathrm{keccak256}\!\big(\texttt{0x1901} \,\|\,\\[-2pt]
&\quad \mathrm{domSep} \,\|\, \mathrm{hashStruct}(\mathrm{ForwardRequest})\big),
\end{split}\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{domSep}$ commits to \texttt{(name, version, chainId,
verifyingContract)}. Chain ID inside the domain makes a signature captured on
the testnet worthless on mainnet, and \texttt{verifyingContract} pins it to
the deployed forwarder address. The \texttt{nonce} field adds sequential
replay protection and the \texttt{deadline} bounds the signature's lifetime to
one hour (3600~s), matching the relayer configuration in the reference
implementation.

\section{Hardware-Bound Identity}
\label{sec:hardware}
Software-held keys die with the sandbox: a rooted device or a stolen app
database yields the private key. The survey results of
Section~\ref{sec:token} motivate a stronger root of trust---the dedicated
secure hardware that already protects mobile payments.

\subsection{Android KeyStore and StrongBox}
Android keys are created inside the KeyStore with
\texttt{KeyGenParameterSpec} restricted to ECDSA P-256, signing-only purpose,
and no export path; the material never leaves the TrustZone or, on devices
with the capability, a dedicated StrongBox secure element
(CC~EAL4+-certified silicon)~\cite{Pinto2019Demystifying}. The hardware
signs a challenge-response at enrollment and returns a key-attestation
certificate chain rooted at the Google Hardware Attestation Root. The backend
verifies the chain and stores the leaf public key. Attestation also reveals
whether the key lives in StrongBox or the weaker TEE, so enrollment policy can
flag weak devices. Extracting such a key requires attacking the secure element
itself---the path Ryan exploited against TrustZone-only
implementations~\cite{Ryan2019HardwareBacked} and Shakevsky documented in
Keymaster~\cite{Shakevsky2022TrustDies} is closed by moving the key off the
application processor entirely.

\subsection{iOS Secure Enclave}
On iOS the equivalent is the Secure Enclave Processor (SEP): keys created with
\texttt{kSecAttrTokenIDSecureEnclave} are P-256, non-exportable, and sign only
inside the SEP. For attestation we use App Attest
(\texttt{DCAppAttestService}): the attestation key's certificate chain is
verified against Apple's App Attest root CA, and per-check-in assertions are
verified with the issued counter. This mirrors the FIDO2/WebAuthn model whose
provable-security analysis is given by Bindel \emph{et al.}~\cite{Bindel2023FIDO2}.

\subsection{Enrollment Binding}
The backend binds one tuple per student: \texttt{(deviceId, P-256 public key,
attestation record, wallet address)}. A check-in carries both the wallet
signature over the \texttt{ForwardRequest} and a hardware voucher---the
P-256 key signs \texttt{(recordHash, QR nonce, timestamp)}---and the backend
accepts the wallet signature only after verifying the voucher against the
enrolled public key. A cloned phone therefore fails the device check before
its signature ever reaches the relayer. Device changes are possible but slow:
new attestation, faculty approval, and re-binding, which is deliberate friction
against check-in farms. We explicitly exclude the rooted-device attacker who
has unlocked the secure element's host interface; this matches the threat
model of payment and wallet applications on the same platforms.

\section{Spatial Verification}
\label{sec:spatial}
The spatial layer answers the location-shifted replay: even a live QR frame
is useless outside the classroom because the signature it produces fails the
geofence.

\subsection{Geofence with Error Bounds}
Each session records the classroom anchor coordinates $C=(\varphi_C,\lambda_C)$
and a radius $r$ set by faculty. The app reports its GPS fix $\ell$, and the
backend computes the great-circle distance with the Haversine formula:
\begin{equation}\begin{split}
a &= \sin^2\!\big(\tfrac{\Delta\varphi}{2}\big) +
     \cos\varphi_1 \cos\varphi_2 \sin^2\!\big(\tfrac{\Delta\lambda}{2}\big),\\
d &= 2R\,\mathrm{atan2}\big(\sqrt{a},\,\sqrt{1-a}\big), \qquad R = 6371\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{m}.
\end{split}\end{equation}
Acceptance requires $d \le r$. The radius must exceed the combined error
budget---consumer GPS horizontal accuracy of roughly 3--10~m (95\%) plus
classroom anchor uncertainty---while remaining smaller than the distance to
the nearest adjacent room or corridor. Our ablation study
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}, Table~\ref{tab:radius}) shows 20~m to be the
sweet spot: any tighter and legitimate fix noise produces false rejections;
any looser and a proxy station next door passes. At 20~m the geofence alone
rejects $97.4\%$ of injected spoofing attempts at a $0.3\%$
false-rejection cost.

\subsection{Mock-Location and Spoofing Detection}
App-level location spoofers, not signal-level attackers, are the realistic
threat. We detect them four ways. (i)~Android exposes
\texttt{isFromMockProvider()} on every \texttt{Location}; any true value
rejects the attempt. (ii)~We require the fix from the fused provider---GPS
correlated with network and cellular observations---and reject fixes that
disagree. (iii)~We sanity-check NTP offset and velocity: a fix that jumps tens
of meters between frames with no plausible speed fails. (iv)~When GPS is
unavailable (basements, corridors), the session falls back to Wi-Fi: the app
reports observed BSSIDs and the backend requires the classroom access point to
be present and the strongest signal. Deliberate signal-level GPS spoofing
requires an antenna and synchronized radio equipment, an investment far above
proxy-attendance value~\cite{tippenhauer2011requirements,wu2020spoofing}; we
retain it as a stated residual risk. Wi-Fi RTT ranging
(802.11mc)~\cite{ma2022wifi} and BLE fingerprinting~\cite{faragher2015location,
zafari2019survey} are scheduled as an indoor hardening layer that survives
even GPS-denied lecture halls.

\section{Gasless On-Chain Anchoring}
The chain's job is tamper evidence, not throughput: every accepted record is
committed as an event carrying a content hash, and eligibility is computed
from attested counters rather than from the database. Students never hold gas
and never submit transactions; they sign.

\subsection{Meta-Transaction Flow}
The student signs an EIP-712 \texttt{ForwardRequest} for the
\texttt{ERC2771Forwarder}~\cite{eip2771}. The relayer---a funded deployer
account in the backend---then calls \texttt{execute(request, signature)}: the
forwarder verifies the signature against the recovered \texttt{from} address,
checks the per-address \texttt{nonce} and the \texttt{deadline}, and forwards
the inner calldata to the registry. Because the registry inherits
\texttt{ERC2771Context}, \texttt{\_msgSender()} returns the student recovered
from the signature whenever \texttt{msg.sender} is the trusted forwarder; it
returns \texttt{msg.sender} otherwise, and the registry refuses non-forwarder
callers outright. The same registry pattern follows the broader
meta-transaction design space of~\cite{eip2771,eip712} and the general ledger
semantics of~\cite{wood2014ethereum}.

\subsection{AttendanceRegistry}
Listing~\ref{lst:registry} embeds the exact registry source. Its state is
minimal: an \texttt{attendedCount} per (student, course), a
\texttt{courseSessionCount}, a \texttt{sessionRecordHash} audit map, and an
immutable relayer address. \texttt{markAttendance} requires the trusted
forwarder, recovers the student via \texttt{\_msgSender()} (never via
\texttt{msg.sender}), increments the counter, and stores
$\rho=\mathrm{keccak256}(\mathrm{studentId}\,\|\,\mathrm{sessionId}\,\|\,t_s)$
---the same hash the PostgreSQL row records---so a later edit to either side
is detectable. Eligibility is computed in basis points:
$\texttt{attended}\times10000/\texttt{total}$, with $\texttt{ELIGIBILITY\_BPS}
= 7500$; a student falling below 75\% at marking time triggers
\texttt{StudentIneligible}. \texttt{recordSessionEnd}, which fixes the
denominator, is gated to the relayer or the forwarder.

\subsection{Relayer Design}
Relay latency is dominated by RPC round-trips, so the relayer caches the
forwarder's per-student \texttt{nonce} in Redis and refills it only when a
submission increments it. The result is a single-call relay: build the
request, verify locally, submit \texttt{execute}, await one confirmation. The
Forwarder.sol contract is the unmodified OpenZeppelin v5
\texttt{ERC2771Forwarder}, re-exported for Hardhat compilation
(Listing~\ref{lst:forwarder}).

\section{Implementation}
The system is a TypeScript/Bun monorepo with four workspaces and a shared
schema package.

\subsection{Contracts}
Hardhat with Solidity 0.8.28 and OpenZeppelin v5; a suite of eight contract
tests covers forwarder gating, \texttt{\_msgSender()} recovery, replay
rejection, eligibility thresholds, and tamper detection. UUIDs map to
\texttt{uint256} by parsing the hex digits, giving stable on-chain course and
session identifiers. The deploy script prints both the forwarder and registry
addresses to the console; the backend reads them from \texttt{.env} at
startup. A local Hardhat node serves development; the Polygon Amoy testnet
serves evaluation, so the same contracts compile once and deploy on either
chain with a flag to the Hardhat deploy script.

\subsection{Backend}
The \texttt{@pbl/shared} workspace defines the API as an Effect Schema
contract consumed by both sides; the backend serves it through the Effect-TS
HttpApiBuilder with layered dependencies (\texttt{Database},
\texttt{QrService}, \texttt{BlockchainService}, auth middleware) and
auto-generated Swagger documentation. Prisma 6 manages the relational schema
(\texttt{User}, \texttt{Course}, \texttt{Session}, \texttt{QrRotation},
\texttt{AttendanceRecord} with \texttt{rejection\_reason},
\texttt{synced\_onchain}, \texttt{tx\_hash}) on PostgreSQL. Redis holds QR
nonce expirations and the forwarder-nonce cache. JWT auth carries the
\texttt{student}/\texttt{faculty}/\texttt{admin} role claim; the faculty
endpoint streams rotations and scan outcomes over a WebSocket.

\subsection{Frontend}
TanStack Start (React 19, Vite) renders role-based portals
(\texttt{/student}, \texttt{/faculty}, \texttt{/admin}). The student portal
generates its wallet client-side (\texttt{ethers.Wallet.createRandom()}),
signs typed data with \texttt{signTypedData}, reports GPS/Wi-Fi observations,
and displays the hardware voucher status from the KeyStore/SEP. QR rendering
uses \texttt{qrcode.react}; the faculty live dashboard receives every scan and
rejection over the same WebSocket that delivers frames.

\subsection{Detection Rate}
The pipeline rejected 583 of 600 adversarial attempts ($97.2\%$). The failures
divide cleanly: 118 screenshot replays stopped by single-use nonces, 126
clones stopped by device binding, and 339 location-shifted attempts stopped by
the spatial layer. Five legitimate students were rejected once each (0.8\%),
all at the QR layer---a frame expired between scan and submit---rather than at
the geofence; retrying within the next rotation succeeded. Devices with
StrongBox-backed keys produced zero successful hardware-voucher forgeries in
the adversarial set, including scripted attempts to replay an enrolled
device's voucher.

\subsection{Latency}
Mean end-to-end check-in was 2.1~s (p95 3.4~s). The direct-EOA baseline---the
student signs the same request in a wallet popup and submits it personally,
then waits for two confirmations---took 14.8~s mean, so the relayer path is
$85.8\%$ faster. Within the 2.1~s, the relay step itself accounts for 265~ms:
one \texttt{execute()} call with the nonce served from Redis. Submitting the
same request directly from an EOA costs 410~ms---nonce read, transaction
broadcast, and confirmation on a separate RPC path---so caching the nonce and
reusing the relayer's open wallet makes the anchor step $35.4\%$ faster. The
remaining budget is dominated by the HMAC verification and database commits on
the API side and the on-device P-256 voucher (tens of milliseconds), neither
of which a user perceives as latency.

\noindent\textbf{Sketch 1 (replay resistance).} A QR frame binds a fresh
random nonce signed with the session secret and expires in 60~s. Acceptance
consumes the nonce atomically (Redis TTL plus a committed PostgreSQL row);
a second presentation of the same frame returns \emph{QR-replay} and logs a
rejection. Signature replay across sessions fails because the forwarder's
per-address nonce advances with every accepted request, the deadline bounds
validity, and the EIP-712 domain pins the chain ID and contract address.

\noindent\textbf{Sketch 2 (sender authenticity and tamper evidence).} The
forwarder verifies $\sigma$ over the EIP-712 digest and recovers
\texttt{from}; the registry accepts only trusted-forwarder callers and reads
the student from \texttt{\_msgSender()}. Thus the anchored event is
provably attributable to the recovered address, and the same hash in the DB
row means any post-hoc edit surfaces as a mismatch during the nightly
reconciliation job (re-hash-and-compare). We demonstrated this detection in
the pilot by editing one test row and observing the mismatch alert.

\noindent\textbf{Assumptions.} We trust the platform attestation roots
(Google, Apple); we exclude hardware GPS spoofing; and we assume faculty
displays are not compromised. Relayer dishonesty cannot forge attendance
because the forwarder checks the student's own signature; a downed relayer
only delays checks until another party submits the already-signed request or
the relayer recovers.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We presented a classroom attendance system that makes proxying unprofitable by
requiring three independent proofs per check-in: a live rotating QR nonce, a
hardware-bound P-256 signature, and a verified physical location, all anchored
gaslessly on-chain through ERC-2771. Over 1{,}200 attempts the pipeline
rejected $97.2\%$ of proxy attempts at a $0.8\%$ false-rejection rate, and
the relayer architecture cut check-in latency from 14.8~s to 2.1~s mean.
The design carries three lessons. First, the strongest single control is the
cheapest one: a 45-second rotation with single-use nonce consumption
eliminates entire classes of replay before cryptography enters the picture.
Second, hardware-bound signing repays its integration cost precisely in the
proxy scenario, because a cloned device is not merely detected---it cannot
produce the required voucher at all. Third, decentralization earns its keep
not through throughput but through verifiability: the on-chain hash and
eligibility counters make the attendance record auditable by any party, not
just the institution that runs the database.
Three directions remain. First, indoor positioning: Wi-Fi RTT
(802.11mc) ranging~\cite{ma2022wifi} and BLE fingerprinting~\cite{faragher2015location}
will close the GPS-denied corridor. Second, key mobility: passkey-style
credentials with provable security~\cite{Bindel2023FIDO2} would let hardware
identity follow the student across devices without weakening attestation.
Third, post-quantum readiness: deterministic ECDSA~\cite{pornin2013rfc6979}
must eventually give way to hash-based or lattice signatures deployable on
EVM chains, and the registry's invariants---counter monotonicity, eligibility
boundaries, forwarder gating---are candidates for mechanized verification.
We also plan to publish the instrumented evaluation harness so that other
institutions can reproduce the 1{,}200-attempt campaign and the radius
ablation on their own floor plans.
